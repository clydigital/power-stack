AI Chips Fall. Is the Market Finally Questioning the Bill?

US stocks barely moved on Tuesday, but semiconductor shares continued to look weaker. The broad US benchmark closed at 7,805.45, down just 0.02%, and the Nasdaq slipped 0.15%. Meanwhile, the semiconductor ETF SMH fell 0.49% to 588.12.

That is quite a contrast. Investors are not fleeing every large company, but they are becoming less willing to pay any price for the chips and hardware behind the AI boom.

Chinese memory-maker CXMT has claimed progress on denser memory technology. That increases competitive risk for Micron and other suppliers if it can scale, but its mass-production economics and competitive impact have not yet been demonstrated.

Where Is the Money Coming From?

Steve Eisman is worried about the way money moves around the AI industry. OpenAI and Anthropic promise to spend heavily on computing power. Cloud companies build data centres for them, and some of those same cloud and chip companies also invest in the labs.

Broadcom chief Hock Tan warned on 12 October that funding, rather than chip supply, could become the main hurdle to AI expansion. His remarks support testing the cash behind large commitments, not declaring that orders have already failed.

The arrangement can work if enough independent customers pay to use the AI. If those payments cannot keep up, someone will eventually have to borrow more, spend less or delay a project.

Big orders are not the same thing as profit. Oracle's backlog is enormous, yet future contracts must still turn into actual cash. On the other hand, Microsoft reported positive free cash flow last quarter, so it would be wrong to say every AI company has already run out of money.

What About the Picks and Shovels?

Power and electrical equipment have a more physical demand story. Data centres need turbines, transformers, switchgear, cooling and fast network connections before anyone can use an AI model.

TSMC's own September filing offers an important counterweight: revenue reached NT$511.86 billion, 54.6% higher than a year earlier. This supports present semiconductor demand without settling whether AI labs will earn enough to pay for future capacity.

That is why GE Vernova, Eaton and network companies such as Arista deserve attention. They can sell equipment to multiple operators. But it is still important to ask whether customers can finance the construction and whether the share price already assumes years of growth.

Micron and Nvidia remain central to the buildout, although the memory and chip trade is much more sensitive to customers slowing their orders. I would rather see current earnings, cash and the share price agree before chasing that weakness.

Why Are Yields Still Important?

Borrowing remains expensive even after bonds found some relief. Reports on Tuesday placed the US 10-year yield around 5.22% and the 30-year near 5.56%. That is a high hurdle for data centres and other businesses relying on money they hope to earn years from now.

Oil has also been volatile because of the Middle East conflict and changing fuel supply expectations. A fresh rise in energy prices could keep inflation sticky, limiting relief for long-term borrowing rates.

What Would Change My View?

The US CPI release is scheduled for Wednesday, 14 October at 20:30 MYT. TSMC reports on Thursday, 15 October at 14:00 MYT, followed by US PPI at 20:30 MYT. Softer inflation matters more if yields and credit conditions respond rather than stocks merely bouncing for an hour.

For the AI trade, I want to see real orders and cash conversion hold up, especially in memory, cloud services and power equipment. If customers start cutting orders while financing costs rise, the warning becomes much more serious.

My view: the AI investment cycle is still active, but the easy part of the trade may be behind us. I would favour financially sound suppliers and wait for a clearer price entry until paid demand and funding are better proven.

TSMC: will AI orders still justify the spending?

15 October, 14:00 MYT · Q3 earnings conference.

As of 14 October, revenue was already reported up about 50% year on year. Previous Q3 guidance was 65–67% gross margin and 56–58% operating margin. These are pre-release figures, not final Q3 margins.

I want to see profitable orders, advanced packaging demand and cash flow hold up. Weaker revenue alone does not prove AI labs cannot pay their bills; it would take verifiable project cuts and worsening funding to raise the concern.
