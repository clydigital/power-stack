Oil Pulls Back. What Does That Mean for Stocks?

Oil changed direction this morning. Brent started the Asian session higher, then fell around 1.3% to $103.35 a barrel by 8:37 a.m. MYT. That gave markets a little relief after another tense weekend for energy supplies.

Traders are hoping more Russian diesel could reach the market following talk of a Russia–Ukraine energy ceasefire. But neither side has confirmed that agreement. Cheaper oil is what the market is pricing in; more fuel actually arriving is something we still have to prove.

And there is another catch. Oil production is gradually returning in the US Gulf, but that does not automatically mean there is more diesel ready to use. Refineries still need to turn crude into fuel, while ships need safe routes to deliver it. If the Strait of Hormuz stays risky, fuel prices can remain high even as crude pulls back.

What Are Stocks Telling Us?

Nasdaq futures were up around 0.5% in the early Reuters snapshot, while S&P 500 futures rose 0.2%. When oil becomes cheaper, investors start hoping inflation could ease. That could eventually take some pressure off interest rates, which is good news for companies relying on growth.

But futures are only an early indication. I would still want to see the actual US session hold its gains, especially in chipmakers and smaller companies. A rally carried by just a few big technology names would not convince me that the wider market is healthy.

The AI Boom Has Two Very Different Sides

TSMC's September revenue rose 54.6% from a year ago, which is a clear sign that demand for advanced chips remains strong. The factories are still selling the hardware needed to build AI systems, so I would not call the investment boom over just yet.

The problem is what happens after those chips are bought. A company can spend billions building data centres, but it still needs enough paying customers to earn that money back. Microsoft continues to generate substantial cash, while heavily financed builders such as CoreWeave face more pressure to prove their returns. Strong chip sales and weak financing can happen at the same time.

Why I Am Still Watching US Yields

The US 10-year Treasury yield last closed at 5.24% on Friday, 9 October. That is important because higher bond yields make borrowing more expensive and raise the bar for what investors should be willing to pay for future company profits.

US bond markets are observing Monday's holiday, so we do not yet have a fresh closing reaction to the fall in oil. I would want to see yields and borrowing conditions actually improve before treating this as a lasting relief rally.

What Comes Next?

The next tests are US CPI on Wednesday, 14 October, and PPI on Thursday, 15 October. Both will help show whether inflation is easing. TSMC also reports quarterly results on Thursday, giving us another look at whether the demand for AI chips is turning into healthy earnings.

For now, I would be more comfortable with the bullish view if oil stays lower, semiconductor shares start outperforming and credit conditions stop getting worse. If oil rebounds while yields remain high and AI lenders become more cautious, I would stay patient rather than chase the bounce.

My view: oil's pullback gives stocks some breathing room, but the market still needs to prove that inflation and borrowing costs are coming down.

Market prices refer to the morning of 12 October, not a live feed. US Treasury yields are from Friday, 9 October.
